\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{The security lesson: observable entropy}
The failure is not that the implementation forgets to sample a permutation. It samples one for each block. The failure is that, for a reachable input symmetry, many sampled permutations become observationally equivalent. The security-relevant quantity is the orbit of the transformed object, not the cardinality of the randomizer's support. A $b!$ implementation choice can therefore contribute only $b$ distinguishable calibration states.

This distinction is broader than \scheme{}, but the attack is not a theorem about all linear obfuscation. A designer must ask which transformations survive after quotienting by input stabilizers, which fixed structure remains across observations, and whether recovery metadata becomes an oracle. In the tested construction, the single-row marker supports the implementation's decoding procedure and also labels states that expose the columns of $S$. Different marker or mixing structures can change the orbit and the identification problem.

\subsection{Mitigation implications}
Three principles follow. First, do not reuse a transform merely because another component is refreshed: the refreshed component may vanish under a chosen input. Second, bind secrets to the smallest practical identity---request, tenant, or cache object---and specify how shared prefixes and multi-turn sessions inherit that identity. Third, evaluate functional recovery and security together. Replacing a sparse marker with a denser object can defeat this decoder while also breaking the intended inverse; failure of the current attack is not evidence of a correct defense.

Request-specific $S,a$ is the simplest intervention suggested by our measurements. It changes the attacker's problem from amortizing one calibration over other requests to calibrating within the victim's secret scope. Yet the correct engineering choice may instead be authenticated encryption when caches are only stored or transported and need not be manipulated while protected. Obfuscation should be justified by an operation that conventional cryptography cannot efficiently support, not by a general claim that transformed vectors are unintelligible.

Secret lifetime must also be considered together with cache lifetime. A request-scoped transform requires the legitimate decoder to retain the association between a cache object and the parameters that protect it. Shared prefixes or a multi-turn session may span several requests, so the term \emph{per request} does not by itself specify a complete integration policy. Our parameter-generation microbenchmark measures neither that state management nor re-encoding of existing cache objects. The observed transfer failure is consequently a mechanism-level intervention whose integration remains to be validated.

The distinction between functional and security validation is particularly important here. A cache transform can interrupt our numerical recovery while also changing legitimate attention outputs. Establishing a usable repair would require checking corresponding K/V rows, masks, and continued inference under the proposed secret-scope policy, followed by attacks matched to that policy. The present work does not claim those checks have been completed. Its contribution is the demonstrated failure of the reused-secret configuration and the bounded evidence about which component refresh interrupts the measured transfer.

\subsection{A measured defense prototype}
\label{sec:veilkv}
As a follow-up design exercise, we implemented a small prototype, VeilKV, that removes the beacon, derives block transforms from a PRF at decode time, pads the final partial block before protection, and uses separate derivation domains for shared prefixes and salted private tails. We report it as a measured defense prototype rather than a secure replacement. In the Qwen2.5-0.5B CPU experiment, FP32 storage produced a maximum K/V round-trip error of $9.2\times10^{-5}$/$3.8\times10^{-6}$; three texts had zero measured logit difference and 50/50 greedy-token agreement, and incremental decoding matched full decoding. The saved implementation and result record are included in the artifact.

The results also define the boundary of the prefix claim. A single stored prefix artifact reused with deterministic chain-derived transforms matched across 24/24 tested blocks, but independently recomputed forward caches matched bitwise in only 2/24 K layers and 1/24 V layers; the corresponding protected-prefix comparison matched 3/48 blocks. We therefore claim compatibility with radix-style reuse of one stored artifact, not bitwise equality for independently recomputed forward passes. The true-prefix confirmation test did not match under the tested cross-shape setting, which adds practical friction to a dictionary attack but is not a security guarantee.

In the five-repetition CPU timing run, VeilKV obfuscation was 0.1738 s per 1,000 tokens in FP32 and 0.1728 s in BF16, compared with 0.2116 s for the KV-Cloak BF16 comparison. Decode was slower: 0.3128 s (FP32) and 0.2963 s (BF16), compared with 0.1594 s. BF16 K storage also had a maximum round-trip error of 0.90--0.95 in the tested matrix, so BF16 deployment correctness remains open. The attack battery found 127/127 distinct probe blocks and salted-dictionary hits of 0/7. Blind cross-request transfer averaged 3.22\%, while the random-row token-hit null was 0.68\%; the cosine-selected mean was 6.25\%. These numbers do not establish zero leakage, and the master key remains static across requests. The prototype therefore supplies design evidence and explicit residual risks, not a complete secure repair.

\subsection{Limitations and external validity}
Our attacker begins after protected-cache observation is available. We do not show a remote API path to another tenant's cache, measure the prevalence of shared secret epochs, or bypass access control. The study concerns the first Value layer and the tested public implementation. Deeper states, Key recovery, architectures with position-dependent Value inputs, arbitrary orthogonal $M$, and marker patterns outside the analyzed orbit require separate arguments.

The output is unordered within a block. A multiset can expose rare values or keywords, but it is weaker than a verbatim prompt. The synthetic value study uses one model, one secret configuration, 30 cases, and an artificial candidate set. Its incomplete retained traces rule out post-hoc analyses that would require all scores or recovered blocks. The official-entry and multi-epoch studies reuse public conversations; neither supports a claim about an unseen deployment distribution.

The main 99.668\% total weights models with more KV heads and texts with more complete blocks more heavily. It is an audit total, not an estimate over a defined population of users. The model and checkpoint breadth nevertheless tests whether the mechanism is tied to one tokenizer or hidden dimension. Claims about prevalence, per-user harm probability, and production cost remain outside the evidence.

\subsection{Ethics, disclosure, and release}
All experiments ran locally on public model checkpoints, synthetic text, or a publicly released conversation dataset~\cite{lmsys}. We did not recruit or interact with human participants, query a live third-party service, access private caches, or recover non-public user data. No separate human-subjects protocol was submitted because the study involved no participant interaction or new private-data collection. The public conversation subset is used only to exercise file-format and recovery behavior; the manuscript does not reproduce conversation text or identifiers.

The attack is dual use because cache-read access could be converted into content leakage. The authors of \scheme{} were notified of this vulnerability via a GitHub issue on September 28, 2026. That initial notice requested a private reporting channel and did not include technical details. The accompanying research artifact contains local experiment scripts and frozen results; its publication makes technical details accessible. The initial notification does not establish maintainer acknowledgment, delivery of a detailed private report, or an agreed release timeline.

The source snapshot, frozen result records and their hashes, and figure/table generation scripts are provided through the anonymous artifact at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/fresh-shuffles-artifact-ED8D/}. The artifact distinguishes exploratory results from records supporting the paper's claims. Any change to a frozen result or denominator requires regenerating the evidence manifest and manuscript assets.
