\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{The security lesson: observable entropy}
The failure is not that the implementation forgets to sample a permutation. It samples one for each block. The failure is that, for a reachable input symmetry, many sampled permutations become observationally equivalent. The security-relevant quantity is the orbit of the transformed object, not the cardinality of the randomizer's support. A $b!$ implementation choice can therefore contribute only $b$ distinguishable calibration states.

This distinction is broader than \scheme{}, but the attack is not a theorem about all linear obfuscation. A designer must ask which transformations survive after quotienting by input stabilizers, which fixed structure remains across observations, and whether recovery metadata becomes an oracle. In the tested construction, the single-row marker supports the implementation's decoding procedure and also labels states that expose the columns of $S$. Different marker or mixing structures can change the orbit and the identification problem.

\subsection{Mitigation implications}
Three principles follow. First, do not reuse a transform merely because another component is refreshed: the refreshed component may vanish under a chosen input. Second, bind secrets to the smallest practical identity---request, tenant, or cache object---and specify how shared prefixes and multi-turn sessions inherit that identity. Third, evaluate functional recovery and security together. Replacing a sparse marker with a denser object can defeat this decoder while also breaking the intended inverse; failure of the current attack is not evidence of a correct defense.

Request-specific $S,a$ is the simplest intervention suggested by our measurements. It changes the attacker's problem from amortizing one calibration over other requests to calibrating within the victim's secret scope. Yet the correct engineering choice may instead be authenticated encryption when caches are only stored or transported and need not be manipulated while protected. Obfuscation should be justified by an operation that conventional cryptography cannot efficiently support, not by a general claim that transformed vectors are unintelligible.

Secret lifetime must also be considered together with cache lifetime. A request-scoped transform requires the legitimate decoder to retain the association between a cache object and the parameters that protect it. Shared prefixes or a multi-turn session may span several requests, so the term \emph{per request} does not by itself specify a complete integration policy. Our parameter-generation microbenchmark measures neither that state management nor re-encoding of existing cache objects. The observed transfer failure is consequently a mechanism-level intervention whose integration remains to be validated.

The distinction between functional and security validation is particularly important here. A cache transform can interrupt our numerical recovery while also changing legitimate attention outputs. Establishing a usable repair would require checking corresponding K/V rows, masks, and continued inference under the proposed secret-scope policy, followed by attacks matched to that policy. The present work does not claim those checks have been completed. Its contribution is the demonstrated failure of the reused-secret configuration and the bounded evidence about which component refresh interrupts the measured transfer.

\subsection{A measured defense prototype}
\label{sec:veilkv}
As a follow-up design exercise, we implemented VeilKV-Plus, a two-profile prototype that removes the beacon, derives block transforms from HKDF-domain-separated material, authenticates cache blocks, and separates public-shareable prefixes from private tails. The TEE profile protects serialized K/V bytes with standard encrypt-then-MAC processing and performs key release through an attestation stub; the arithmetic profile retains a reversible floating-point mixing layer for settings without byte-level decryption. We report this as a defense candidate under the simulated TEE boundary, not as a proof of a deployed TEE system. The v4 run covers Qwen2.5-0.5B and Llama-3.2-1B, retains run-1 attack and performance evidence, and records all stage outputs in the artifact.

The prefix result is now defined at the artifact-manager boundary. A public prefix artifact is authenticated with block-scoped metadata and can be reused across requests; the private tail starts from the terminal tag of the reused prefix chain. In the v4 paged harness, cross-request prefix byte identity was $1/1$, chain-aware prefix-to-tail decoding matched the unprotected BF16 baseline (K error $0.49898$, V error $0.0312$), and greedy decoding matched for 20 steps. Wrong terminal tags, wrong principals, block swaps, truncation, eviction/reload, relocation, and page substitution were detected. The guarantee concerns reuse of one stored artifact; independently recomputed forward caches can still differ because deeper-layer floating-point reductions depend on the request shape.

The v4 integrity battery detected all 12 tested manipulations, including replay after epoch changes, block substitution, block swapping, truncation, AAD changes, and cross-tenant replacement. Private closed-set dictionary attempts produced $0/100$ hits for both evaluated models, while the public-prefix equality oracle remains an intentional policy-bound leakage. The persistent nonce store rejected reuse after a simulated service restart. Attestation remains a stub, so the result establishes the cache protocol and its failure handling rather than the security of a particular hardware TEE.

The TEE profile had protect/recover medians of 0.1159/0.0987 seconds per 1,000 tokens in the CPU benchmark inherited by v4, compared with 0.2238/0.4142 seconds for the KV-Cloak comparison. These are approximately $1.93\times$ and $4.20\times$ reductions in the measured operation time against the benchmark's \texttt{fused=False} baseline, not the published weight-fused serving path. The benchmark measures cache protection and recovery; it excludes hardware attestation and does not establish an end-to-end serving speedup.

A no-sharing authenticated-encryption baseline provides a closer comparison for the cost of this integration. Its median protection time is 0.1091 seconds per 1,000 tokens, versus 0.1159 for the TEE profile: the latter pays approximately 6.2\% more for protection in this benchmark. The saved record does not provide a corresponding no-sharing recovery measurement. Thus the supported advantage over that baseline is the demonstrated reuse of one policy-public prefix artifact with an authenticated private continuation, rather than faster encryption or stronger cryptographic primitives. This does not establish that other encryption-based systems cannot support sharing. Our reuse test comprises one cross-request artifact pair and a paged harness, not a production hit-rate or throughput study.

The public/private distinction is essential to this advantage. A public prefix's equality remains observable by design, and its reuse cannot be advertised as confidentiality for arbitrary low-entropy private prefixes. Private-tail dictionary attempts instead exercise principal- and nonce-scoped protection under the simulated TEE assumption. The stored-artifact test also differs from independently recomputing a prefix: deeper-layer floating-point reductions can change with request shape. Those differences are a reproducibility constraint, not a security guarantee.

\subsection{Negative result for the arithmetic profile}
The subsequent optimization study resolves the earlier low-power spectral-leakage concern. Its retained per-block records contain 100 blocks per model and 100 candidates per identification task. Gram-based top-1 identification succeeds on 23/100 Qwen2.5-0.5B blocks and 38/100 Llama-3.2-1B blocks; the recorded 95\% Wilson intervals are [15.84\%,32.15\%] and [29.10\%,47.79\%]. Whitening succeeds on 18/100 and 6/100, respectively. The uniform candidate baseline is 1\%, while the sampled random controls obtain 0/100 and 2/100. These observations support a closed-set identification leak in the arithmetic profile; they do not measure exact prompt reconstruction or deployment-wide leakage rates.

Optimization improves arithmetic protection/recovery means to 0.7325/0.5789 seconds per 1,000 tokens in the final longer-input benchmark (464 actual tokens). The earlier approximately 11.4-second figure came from a short-input benchmark and cannot serve as the same-input baseline for a speedup claim. In the final benchmark, the recorded KV-Cloak means are 0.0596/0.0969 seconds per 1,000 tokens. Performance therefore remains unfavorable, and the identification result independently excludes the arithmetic profile from confidentiality claims. Improving numerical reversibility or runtime does not remove its observed leakage. The TEE-profile evidence remains scoped to byte protection under a simulated, static master key and an unimplemented hardware attestation protocol.

\subsection{Limitations and external validity}
Our attacker begins after protected-cache observation is available. We do not show a remote API path to another tenant's cache, measure the prevalence of shared secret epochs, or bypass access control. The study concerns the first Value layer and the tested public implementation. Deeper states, Key recovery, architectures with position-dependent Value inputs, arbitrary orthogonal $M$, and marker patterns outside the analyzed orbit require separate arguments.

The direct output is unordered within a block. Language-prior sorting gives partial sequence estimates, while the direction-pair diagnostic does not control blockwise bags. Neither observation establishes zero factual leakage or general verbatim reconstruction. The synthetic value study uses one model, one secret configuration, 30 cases, and an artificial candidate set. Its incomplete retained traces rule out post-hoc analyses that would require all scores or recovered blocks. The official-entry and multi-epoch studies reuse public conversations; neither supports a claim about an unseen deployment distribution.

The main 99.668\% total weights models with more KV heads and texts with more complete blocks more heavily. It is an audit total, not an estimate over a defined population of users. The model and checkpoint breadth nevertheless tests whether the mechanism is tied to one tokenizer or hidden dimension. Claims about prevalence, per-user harm probability, and production cost remain outside the evidence.

\subsection{Ethics, disclosure, and release}
All experiments ran locally on public model checkpoints, synthetic text, or a publicly released conversation dataset~\cite{lmsys}. We did not recruit or interact with human participants, query a live third-party service, access private caches, or recover non-public user data. No separate human-subjects protocol was submitted because the study involved no participant interaction or new private-data collection. The public conversation subset is used only to exercise file-format and recovery behavior; the manuscript does not reproduce conversation text or identifiers.

The attack is dual use because cache-read access could be converted into content leakage. The authors of \scheme{} were notified of this vulnerability via a GitHub issue on September 28, 2026. That initial notice requested a private reporting channel and did not include technical details. The accompanying research artifact contains local experiment scripts and frozen results; its publication makes technical details accessible. The initial notification does not establish maintainer acknowledgment, delivery of a detailed private report, or an agreed release timeline.

The source snapshot, frozen result records and their hashes, and figure/table generation scripts are provided through the anonymous artifact at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/fresh-shuffles-artifact-ED8D/}. The artifact distinguishes exploratory results from records supporting the paper's claims. Any change to a frozen result or denominator requires regenerating the evidence manifest and manuscript assets.
